This video features *Sophia Sharif*, a 7-time hackathon winner and former tech director of *LA Hacks*, sharing her expert tips on how to win hackathons. The advice focuses on understanding how judges evaluate projects and effectively presenting your work. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DizrlqWIEWs

### Key Takeaways:

*   **Judging Dynamics:** General hackathon prizes are typically judged based on a brief 2-5 minute demo (1:05-1:16). Projects are often evaluated in a head-to-head, "ELO-style" system (1:28-1:49), prioritizing **technical complexity**, **real-world impact**, and **project completion** (1:50-2:02).
*   **Ideation & Planning:** Instead of focusing on abstract concepts, evaluate ideas by imagining the final demo path (2:35-3:08). Build your presentation like a story—introduce a problem, build tension, and present the project as the solution (3:55-4:44).
*   **Execution Strategies:**
    *   **Prioritize features:** Focus on essential functionality first and leave "nice-to-have" features for later (4:53-5:05).
    *   **Manage scope:** Use libraries to handle complex tasks to increase perceived technical depth without needing to code everything from scratch (5:38-5:58).
    *   **Visuals matter:** Incorporate maps, hardware, or diagrams to communicate complexity quickly (5:59-6:24).
    *   **Adaptability:** Don't be a perfectionist; make necessary simplifications when you encounter bugs (6:38-6:51).
*   **The Demo Video:** Always prioritize finishing your demo video in the final hours (7:13-7:30). Since judges and sponsors have limited time to review submissions, a strong video is often the only way they will truly understand your project (7:36-8:00).

The book is Made to Stick by Chip and Dan Heath. Its framework is called SUCCESs: six common factors in sticky ideas: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. These are the parts that matter most for a hackathon pitch:  (supersummary)
Simple: one core message. Pick the one thing judges must remember. If you can't say it in a sentence, you have two pitches. The book also names the "curse of knowledge": you've lived inside your project for 48 hours, so what feels obvious to you isn't obvious to them.
Concrete: show one real user and one real moment. "Reduces moderation time" is forgettable. "Maria reviews 400 flagged posts a shift; ours cuts that to 90" is not. Live demos are the strongest form of concrete.
Unexpected: open with a pattern break. Start with a surprising number, or a failure of the status quo, instead of "Hi, we're team X and we built...".
Credible: prove it works. Use a working demo, a real test result, or a number from an actual run. At a hackathon this beats any authority claim.
Stories: a mini problem → struggle → resolution arc fits in 30 seconds. Don't tell a long origin story.
